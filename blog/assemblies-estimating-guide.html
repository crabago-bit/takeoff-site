Assemblies:
why they beat raw quantities

A takeoff that produces "4,200 SF of wall" still isn't an estimate. One that produces studs, track, board, screws, mud, and labor hours — priced — nearly is. That's what assemblies do.

The gap between a quantity and a cost

Measure a partition wall and takeoff software will happily tell you it's 4,200 square feet. Useful — and nowhere near a bid. Between that quantity and a number you can sign your name to sits a pile of arithmetic: how many studs per square foot of wall, how much track, how many sheets of board per side, screws, mud, tape, a waste allowance on each, a labor rate, and a production rate. Do that math by hand in a spreadsheet for every wall type on every bid and two things happen: you burn evenings, and eventually you fat-finger a cell.

An assembly is that arithmetic, written down once and reused forever. It's a recipe: for one unit of measured quantity, here is everything it takes to build it. Measure the quantity, and the recipe scales.

Anatomy of an assembly

Every assembly, in any system, has three kinds of ingredients:

  • Materials — each with a consumption rate per unit of measurement (studs per SF, gallons per SF, pieces per LF) and a unit cost.
  • Labor — production expressed as hours per unit, multiplied by a loaded labor rate. This is the ingredient spreadsheet-only shops most often estimate by gut feel, and it's usually the biggest line.
  • Waste factors — a percentage on top of each material's theoretical quantity. Board gets cut, studs get culled, mud dries in the bucket. Waste isn't padding; it's physics. Typical factors run from a few percent on framing to 10–15% on sheet goods with lots of cuts, but yours should come from your own jobs.

Change the board price and every wall in the estimate updates. That's the trade: build the recipe once, and every future bid inherits it.

Notice where the money is: in this example labor is over half the assembly cost. Teams that agonize over material pricing but guess at production rates are polishing the small half of the number. Track your crews' actual hours per unit — it's the highest-leverage data an estimating department owns.

Organize the library by CSI division

Ten assemblies need no organization. A hundred and fifty need a filing system, and the industry already has one: CSI MasterFormat divisions. Partitions live under 09, concrete under 03, doors under 08. Organizing your assembly library by division does three quiet things:

  • Findability. Any estimator — including the one you hire next year — knows where drywall assemblies live without asking.
  • Scope checking. Sorting an estimate by division makes holes visible. A tenant-improvement bid with nothing in Division 09 is telling you something.
  • Clean handoff. Your export lands in a structure GCs, subs, and accounting systems already speak.

Keep the costs current — or the recipes rot

An assembly library is a garden, not a monument. Material prices drift; labor rates change every agreement; production rates improve with better crews. A library priced in 2023 will quietly poison every 2026 bid it touches. The sustainable habit is small and regular: after each awarded job, compare a handful of assembly costs against what the job actually cost, and adjust. Quarterly, sweep the unit prices on your ten most-used assemblies against current supplier quotes. An hour a quarter keeps a library trustworthy; skipping it for two years means rebuilding from scratch.

The arm-and-draw workflow

Here's where assemblies change the shape of the workday, and where the tooling matters. The traditional sequence is: measure everything, export quantities, then price in a spreadsheet — takeoff and estimating as two separate jobs, often two separate days. The assembly-first sequence inverts it: arm a tool with an assembly, then draw.

In Groundwork Takeoff, that's literally the mechanic. You pick the partition assembly, trace the wall with the Linear tool (one of six measurement modes, with snap and crosshair keeping the trace honest), and the live estimate grid prices it as the line lands — materials, labor hours, waste, extension, all of it. The Assembly Builder is where the recipe above gets defined: components, consumption rates, waste factors, labor. There's a formula evaluator for the relationships that aren't simple multiplication, and the materials catalog underneath is organized by CSI division, so the library structure argued for above is the default, not a chore.

The practical difference shows up on bid day: when the takeoff is done, the estimate is done too. Review the grid, adjust, and export a bid-ready sheet to Excel — no re-typing quantities into a pricing workbook, which is where a depressing share of estimating errors are born.

Start with five

Don't try to build a 200-assembly library in a weekend. List the five scopes you measure on almost every bid — for a drywall contractor that might be three partition types, a ceiling, and a column wrap. Build those five carefully, use them on the next real bid, and fix what the bid teaches you. A library grows one honest recipe at a time; that's also how it stays trustworthy.
